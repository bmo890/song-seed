# Put the click and the song on one clock

The founder's mismatch is most likely a playback problem, not a grid problem. After every click take, SongNook already lines the saved grid up with the clicks the mic actually recorded. That check runs on Android too, and in the one measured case it agreed with an outside tool to 0.4 ms. The play-along click has no such check. It is a second player that guesses where the song is, and the code shows three certain faults: it reads a playhead that can be up to a second old, it starts late by however long its own setup takes, and it assumes the click and the song leave the speaker with equal delay, which the app's own Bluetooth measurements contradict. On Android there is a second strong suspect that nobody has checked. When an Android take is trimmed and saved, the encoder may leave 23–48 ms of silent padding at the start of the file. If it does, every recorded click plays that much late against a grid that is otherwise correct. A 30-minute file check settles this. The haptic "always lags" for reasons the code can show: each buzz is timed from when the engine *noticed* the previous beat, not from when that beat was due, and the motor delays it assumes are guesses. Research on hearing puts the audible flam between two clicks at about 15–20 ms. So the play-along click should be held to 5 ms, and only one design gets there reliably: render the click inside the same audio stream as the song. The plan below starts with a day of cheap measurements, then over-the-air fixes for the certain bugs, then one native build that makes the click and the song share one clock.

## The recording grid is checked; the play-along click is not

Some background first. A phone has several clocks: the wall clock, the clock that counts samples the speaker has played, the clock that stamps samples the mic has captured, the video player's own position, and React's view of that position. Every timing bug in SongNook happens where a time is copied from one clock to another and something is lost on the way. The code audit mapped every one of those crossings (Appendix A1). It found something lopsided.

**The recording side has a ground-truth step.** After each click take, the app scans the recorded audio for the click bleed, finds where the clicks really landed, and shifts the saved grid onto them. With the metronome on the phone speaker, the bleed is loud. One real take measured a click-to-noise contrast of 30, far above the app's 1.35 gate. In the one take checked against the repo's outside tool, the app and the tool agreed to **0.4 ms**. That was in the iOS simulator. No Android take has ever been checked this way. By design, the step leaves up to 6 ms uncorrected (it ignores corrections smaller than that). So for a speaker take, the grid should sit within about 6 ms of the recorded clicks, *measured on the raw audio before it is saved* (code audit, A2).

That last phrase matters. **The check measures the audio before encoding, not the file that is played back.** On Android, the take is then trimmed and re-encoded to AAC by Google's media3 Transformer. AAC encoders add a short run of "priming" samples at the start, typically 1024 to 2112 samples, which is **23 to 48 ms** at 44.1 kHz. A well-formed file carries a note (an "edit list") telling the player to skip them. Apple's decoders skip them even without the note. On iPhone, SongNook's takes were measured and do carry the note. On Android, nobody has checked whether Transformer writes it, or whether ExoPlayer honours it. If it does not, every recorded click plays 23–48 ms late against a correct grid. The repo's Mac test tool would hide this, because it decodes the file the Apple way (code audit, A3). This one candidate fits all three of the founder's words: small, constant, Android.

**The playback side has no ground-truth step, and it has certain faults.** The play-along click is the metronome engine, started separately from the song's player, at a beat phase computed from the song's position. Three things go wrong. First, the position it reads is React's copy. React only updates that copy when the displayed second changes, so it can be **up to about one second old**. A drift check runs every 10 seconds. It compares the click to that stale position. If they differ by more than 35 ms, it restarts the click *at the stale position's phase*. If staleness is spread evenly from 0 to 1 s, about 96% of drift checks should trigger a restart to a wrong phase. That should cause audible hops every 10 seconds, not a steady small offset, unless the founder listened only to the first 10 seconds. Second, the click reads the position, then waits for several calls into native code (and, with haptics on, an extra latency probe that builds and destroys an Android audio track), then starts. Nothing advances the phase for that wait. The audit estimates **10–60 ms late**, unmeasured. Third, the code assumes "click and song share the output route, both delayed equally." They are not equal. The click's engine counts samples *before* the speaker, while ExoPlayer reports what is *being heard*. On a Samsung phone with AirPods, the app's own calibration logs recorded about **500 ms for the player path and 200 ms for the click path**. On the speaker, the gap is unmeasured; the audit infers tens of ms, click late (code audit, A4).

### A ranked list of suspects

| Rank | Suspect | Status | Size and direction | Cheapest test |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Android AAC padding not skipped on playback | Unchecked | Constant, 23–48 ms. Recorded click late. Every take, every play. | Inspect an exported take for an edit list; decode it two ways and compare (Step 1, M1) |
| 2 | Play-along click starts late and in the wrong "domain" | Certain in code, size unknown | Constant per play, tens of ms. Play-along click likely late on speaker; about 300 ms early on Bluetooth | Record the speaker during playback with a second device (M3) |
| 3 | Drift check restarts the click at a stale position | Certain in code | Hops of up to ~1 s (wrapped to the bar) every 10 s | Logcat `[timing] click drift` / `click sync` lines (M2) |
| 4 | Saved grid off the recorded clicks (self-check declined) | Possible | Tens of ms, either sign | Recording-side log lines plus the grid-truth tool (M6) |
| 5 | Drift tolerance of 35 ms | By design | Up to 35 ms tolerated | Same as rank 2, over 60 s |
| 6 | Count-in hand-off starts the song a beat early, then resyncs | Certain in code | Only with count-in on | Play with and without count-in |
| 7 | Rare race that cuts a whole bar off the head | Rare (~0.3–0.5% of takes) | One bar | Visible on the take |

**A warning about fixing these one at a time.** Ranks 1 and 2 probably push in the *same* direction on paper but cancel in the ear. Padding makes the recorded click late. The late start makes the play-along click late too. What the founder hears is the difference. Fix only one of them and the mismatch could get *worse*. This is exactly why the app keeps chasing small timing bugs: each fix changes one term in a sum nobody has measured end to end. The measurement in M3 is the cure, because it measures the sum.

## The haptic is late for reasons the code can show

The founder says the haptic cue always lags the click. The audit found several delays that are always in the late direction, and none that push it early.

**Each buzz is timed from the wrong moment.** The engine checks every 8 ms whether a beat has played. When it notices beat *k*, it schedules the buzz for beat *k+1* at "now plus one beat." But "now" is when it noticed, not when beat *k* was due. The engine knows the exact due time from its sample count, and throws it away. The audit estimates **5–15 ms late on average, up to about 25 ms, with jitter** (code audit, A5). This is the top suspect on both platforms.

**On iPhone, the buzz uses the slowest path.** Every beat creates a fresh haptic generator and fires it on the main UI thread, which is busy redrawing the beat display at that exact moment. Apple's documentation says the Taptic Engine "needs time to prepare," and that a generator made ready with `prepare()` ahead of time has "lower latency." Preparing just before firing does not help ([Apple: prepare()](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/uifeedbackgenerator/prepare())). SongNook never prepares one. The code audit also found that iOS's audio session has a switch that allows haptics while recording, and that the app never sets it. If iOS mutes haptics during recording by default, the recorder's haptic may be missing rather than late. This is unverified on a device; one minute with an iPhone settles it.

**On Android, the buzz is a long one, and its delay is a guess.** The app fires a 28–70 ms one-shot vibration and assumes the motor takes 60 ms to respond (35 ms on iOS). Those constants are labelled in the code as defaults to refine later; nothing measured them. Google's haptics guide says a crisp click should last 10–20 ms, that the motor "may continue to ring for another 20 to 50 milliseconds" after a 20 ms pulse, and that apps should avoid single-shot vibrations for this kind of feedback and prefer the built-in click effects ([Android haptics principles](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/haptics/haptics-principles)). Google's hardware guide treats a single impulse longer than 20 ms as "buzzy" ([AOSP haptics hardware](https://source.android.com/docs/core/interaction/haptics/hardware)). A long buzz is felt at its peak, not its start, while the click has a 3 ms attack. That alone makes a buzz *feel* late. Older vibration motors (ERM) take 50–100 ms to spin up ([TI app note, 2012](https://edgeworker.ti.com/cn/lit/pdf/zhct181)).

**A wrong speaker-latency estimate makes it worse.** The haptic aims at "click leaves the engine + estimated speaker delay − assumed motor delay." If the phone over-reports the speaker delay, the buzz lands late by the excess. The engine's own notes record one Android route reporting **209 ms when the real figure was 108 ms**. If the estimate is unknown, the app uses 0 and the buzz goes early instead (code audit, A5).

**The visual cue uses a different, later path.** The recorder's beat bar and breath, and the Metronome page's dots, light up when a beat *event* crosses from native code into JavaScript and React redraws. That event is never early and often late. A scheduler that fires ahead of time does exist, but on the Metronome page it re-flashes the *previous* dot; the right dot lights only when the late event arrives. So the haptic and the visual come from two different pipelines with two different errors. If the founder judges the haptic against the dots, the errors partly cancel; against the sound, they do not. The app's own acceptance bar, "beep, visual and haptic within about 20 ms of each other, filmed at 240 fps," has never been run (code audit, A5).

Neither platform can tell an app when a vibration actually started. iOS can at least *schedule* one: Core Haptics plays a pattern at an exact engine time with `start(atTime:)` ([Apple: start(atTime:)](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/corehaptics/chhapticpatternplayer/start(attime:)); [Apple: CHHapticEngine](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/corehaptics/chhapticengine)). Android has no scheduling call. On newer devices that support it, Android can derive haptics from the audio stream itself (`HapticGenerator`, API 31), which is the only way to lock a buzz to sound there ([Android: HapticGenerator](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/media/audiofx/HapticGenerator)). For visuals, both platforms tell an app when the *next frame* will reach the screen, on the same clock the audio uses: `CADisplayLink.targetTimestamp` on iOS ([Apple: targetTimestamp](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/quartzcore/cadisplaylink/targettimestamp)) and the Choreographer's expected presentation time on Android ([NDK Choreographer](https://developer.android.com/ndk/reference/group/choreographer)). Lighting the pulse on the frame nearest each beat's sound time gives about ±8 ms at 60 Hz, or ±4 ms at 120 Hz.

## Musicians hear a 15–20 ms flam, so hold the click to 5 ms

The targets should come from what people can hear, with margin. The research is consistent. Two sounds stop seeming simultaneous at about **20 ms for pure tones and 30 ms for piano tones**, and trained listeners can tell which came first at **15–20 ms** ([PLOS ONE review, PMC6561579](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6561579)). These figures come from a review's summary; the original studies were not checked. A click has the sharpest possible attack, so the low end applies. In a steady train of clicks, listeners notice a single displaced beat at about **6 ms** for fast pulses, or **2.5% of the beat** for slower ones. That is about 12.5 ms at 120 BPM. Musical training did not change it ([Friberg & Sundberg 1995](https://typeset.io/pdf/time-discrimination-in-a-monotonic-isochronous-sequence-ynslrz5yhj.pdf)).

**Wobble is worse than a steady offset.** For musicians playing through a system, 10 ms of delay with no wobble was acceptable. But 20 ms, *or 10 ms wobbling by ±3 ms*, made the experience significantly worse ([Jack et al. 2016](https://instrumentslab.org/data/andrew/jack_am2016.pdf)). The classic target for instruments is 10 ms with about 1 ms of wobble ([Wessel & Wright](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.01570)). A steady offset can be calibrated away. A wobbling one cannot. So every test below measures the spread, not only the average.

**Players are far less precise than the grid needs to be, and that is fine.** People tapping along to a beat land tens of ms early on average ([Repp & Su 2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-012-0371-2)). Players also correct for timing shifts they do not consciously hear ([Repp 2001, via reference list](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.1080/00222895.2011.561377)). So a click that is slightly off still pulls the musician's timing. The grid's job is not to match the player's precision; it is to never be the thing that is wrong.

Real users notice. Developers on Apple's audio list report two separately started players running **about 1/10 s apart** ([coreaudio-api 2008](https://lists.apple.com/archives/coreaudio-api/2008/Oct/msg00065.html)). Users of iOS music hosts tune offsets between apps to a precision of about 16 ms and ask developers for offset settings ([Loopy Pro forum](https://forum.loopypro.com/discussion/comment/1444314/)). Moises keeps a help article titled "The Metronome is out of sync" ([Moises help](https://help.moises.ai/hc/en-us/articles/360017718460-The-Metronome-is-out-of-sync)). Complaints about other apps describe drift over time and lag at loop points ([MobileSheets forum](https://www.zubersoft.com/mobilesheets/forum/thread-4363.html); [Beat Note](https://beat-note.canny.io/bugs/p/the-playback-note-highlight-bar-really-lags); [Cubasis forum](https://forums.steinberg.net/t/cubasis-metronome-not-syncing/661207)). No study links timing error to ratings or churn; this is anecdote.

### Accuracy targets for SongNook

| What is compared | Target (average) | Target (spread) | Why this number |
|---|---|---|---|
| Play-along click vs the song and its recorded click, any route | **≤ 5 ms** | ≤ 1–2 ms; no drift over 10 min; no hops at seek, loop or speed change | 3× margin under the 15–20 ms flam; under the 6 ms displaced-beat threshold |
| Saved grid vs recorded clicks, speaker take | **≤ 5 ms** | ≤ 2 ms across takes | Desktop studios get to a few samples after compensation; the 6 ms self-check floor should shrink to match |
| Saved grid, wired headphones (no bleed to check against) | ≤ 10 ms | ≤ 3 ms | OS figures are reliable on wired routes; edge of audible flam |
| Saved grid, Bluetooth with a fresh calibration | ≤ 20 ms, and the app says so | — | Bluetooth delay drifts by tens of ms in one session (below) |
| Haptic vs audible click | **within ±15 ms**, never consistently late | ≤ 8 ms | App's own ≤ 20 ms bar; a weak source puts the audio-touch window at ~20 ms ([blog, low confidence](https://omr.it.com/blog/haptic-feedback-timing-audio-tactile-synchronization/)); research treats ~45 ms as clearly felt ([VibraForge](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.17420)) |
| Visual pulse vs audible click | ≤ 1 frame (≈ 17 ms at 60 Hz) | ≤ 8 ms | Audio-visual tolerance is wider than audio-audio; frame scheduling bounds it |

The haptic and visual numbers rest on weaker evidence than the audio numbers. Treat them as working targets, to be tightened once filmed.

## Other apps calibrate; the platforms already offer one clock

**Android music apps measure the phone with a beep.** n-Track plays a burst through the speaker, records it with the mic, and lines the two up. It prompts this the first time you record, and it has published crowd results for 14,173 devices ([n-Track guide](https://ntrack.com/lk/audio-issues); [n-Track device chart](https://www.ntrack.com/android-latency/devices)). FL Studio Mobile does the same with "a single 'beep'," says Android delays are "usually between 40 and 200 ms," and calls calibration "Not necessary on iOS devices" ([FL Studio Mobile manual](https://www.image-line.com/fl-studio-learning/fl-studio-mobile-online-manual/html/plugins/FL%20Studio%20Mobile_HomePanel_Settings.htm)). BandLab offers a latency test and a manual offset, and calls 100–250 ms "high but might be expected on some devices" ([BandLab help](https://help.bandlab.com/hc/en-us/articles/115002959414-How-do-I-fix-latency-), from search extracts). Cubasis has an offset slider and a calibrate button ([Steinberg forum](https://forums.steinberg.net/t/is-there-some-advice-for-the-cubasis-3-recording-offset-compensation/745813)). **iOS apps mostly trust the numbers iOS reports.** That mostly works, but GarageBand users report takes landing both early and late, and GarageBand offers no manual offset ([Apple Community](https://discussions.apple.com/thread/8306871); [Gear Forum](https://thegearforum.com/threads/ipad-recording-latency%E2%80%A6-help-me.9698/latest)).

Desktop studios follow one rule: **move the recorded audio, keep the grid.** Logic, Ableton and Reaper shift a new take earlier by the measured round trip, and let users correct the driver's figure with a loopback test ([reaper.blog](https://reaper.blog/2018/11/rec_latency_offset/); [Ableton DEC](https://help.ableton.com/hc/en-us/articles/115000234830)). SongNook moves the grid instead of the audio. That is fine for one take on its own. It matters for layers and multi-take work, where every track must share one grid.

**No app was found that checks each take against its own click bleed.** SongNook's self-check is ahead of the field here. The research notes one weakness: a steady click repeats every beat, so a match is only unique within one beat. SongNook already works within one beat, which is why it cannot see the rare whole-bar error. Google's own loopback test uses noise bursts, not clicks, so the start is unambiguous ([AOSP: measure latency](https://source.android.com/docs/core/audio/latency/measure)).

**Bluetooth is handled with advice, not engineering.** Apps say "use wired headphones" ([n-Track guide](https://ntrack.com/lk/audio-issues)). No app was found that refuses to record over Bluetooth or calibrates it per route. The numbers explain why. One developer measured AirPods 2 on an iPhone 12 mini: iOS reported 161 ms, but the real figure was **215–220 ms just after connecting, falling to 155–160 ms over 20–30 minutes** ([Apple forums 679274](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/679274)). Another saw the delay move from 193 to 260 ms within a minute ([Apple forums 126277](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/126277)). Lab tests put standard SBC Bluetooth at 173 ms and aptX Low Latency at 34 ms ([RTINGS](https://www.rtings.com/headphones/learn/latency)). A speaker beep test cannot measure earbuds, because the phone's mic cannot hear them. So Bluetooth needs a tap test like SongNook's, a fresh one per session, and honest labelling.

**The platforms already provide a shared clock.** On Android, `AudioTrack.getTimestamp` gives the time a given sample is *heard*, on the same monotonic clock that `AudioRecord.getTimestamp` uses for the mic. Google's compatibility rules *strongly recommend*, but do not require, ±1 ms accuracy ([AOSP CDD commit](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/compatibility/cdd/+/f845c6c%5E%21/); [AOSP AudioTrack.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/aosp-mirror/platform_frameworks_base/main/media/java/android/media/AudioTrack.java)). The hidden `getLatency` call that SongNook uses carries a note from Google: "DO NOT UNHIDE… too many problems." Google also states there is "no API to determine audio latency over any path" at runtime ([NDK audio latency](https://developer.android.com/ndk/guides/audio/audio-latency)). ExoPlayer's own position uses `getTimestamp` when it can ([Media3 AudioTrackPositionTracker](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/androidx/media/release/libraries/exoplayer/src/main/java/androidx/media3/exoplayer/audio/AudioTrackPositionTracker.java)). Most important, Media3 lets an app insert its own **audio processor** into the player's stream, and tells it the media position its samples start from ([Media3 StreamMetadata](https://developer.android.com/reference/androidx/media3/common/audio/AudioProcessor.StreamMetadata)). A click mixed in there is locked to the song to the sample, whatever the speaker delay. It must sit *before* the speed and pitch stage, so it counts song time rather than output time. On iOS, everything runs on one host clock, and players inside one `AVAudioEngine` can start together at a shared future time ([Apple forums 14138](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/14138)).

Practice tools that click over recordings work this way too. Transcribe! places beats as markers *on the recording* and clicks when playback passes one, so the click follows any speed change for free ([Transcribe! 8.73](https://seventhstring.com/xscribe/history/history873.html)). Moises appears to deliver its click as a pre-made audio track played as a stem, but that page could not be read, so treat it as unconfirmed ([Moises help](https://help.moises.ai/hc/en-us/articles/360017718460-The-Metronome-is-out-of-sync)).

Two undeclared delays are worth knowing. On iOS, the default audio mode adds **about 30 ms** of mic processing that "measurement" mode removes ([Superpowered](https://superpowered.com/latency)). On Android, mic noise-cancelling added about 13 ms on one device ([Superpowered media server](https://superpowered.com/superpowered-android-media-server)). Both are dated vendor figures. SongNook's iOS simulator measured an unexplained +30 ms on speaker layers. It is the same size, but that is a coincidence until measured on a phone.

## What this means for SongNook

The pattern behind "chasing small timing bugs one by one" is clear now. Recording has a measurement at the end, so its errors get caught. Playback, haptics and visuals have none, so each fix moves one term in an unmeasured sum. The plan has three parts. First, measure the sum. Then remove the certain bugs cheaply. Then remove the clock crossings themselves, so there is nothing left to drift.

### Step 1: measure first (this week, no build needed)

### Step 2: fix the certain bugs over the air (JavaScript only)

These change only JavaScript and ship as an update. They remove the large, certain errors. They cannot reach 5 ms on their own, because the click is still a second player guessing at the song. Expect them to get the play-along click to roughly 10–20 ms on speaker; that estimate is unmeasured.

| Order | Fix | Removes | Ships |
|---|---|---|---|
| O1 | **Read a fresh playhead.** Feed the click the per-tick position the player already sends (used today for loop wraps), and add the time since that tick. Run the drift check on it, and resync only after two checks agree. | Suspect 3 (hops every 10 s) | OTA |
| O2 | **Stop starting late.** Move the latency probe and haptic setup out of the start path (cache them). Read the position *immediately* before starting the click, and advance the phase by any wait that remains. | Most of suspect 2's start delay | OTA |

| O4 | **Hand-offs.** Start the song on the downbeat after a count-in, not on the last count-in click. After a seek, wait until the player reports playing at the target before restarting the click. | Suspect 6 and seek seams | OTA |

### Step 4: one clock (the structural fix)

This is the step that ends the bug class. **Render the play-along click inside the song's own audio stream.** On Android, add a Media3 audio processor before the speed and pitch stage. It knows the song position of every sample it handles, and mixes a click sample at each beat. On iOS, play the click from a node in the same `AVAudioEngine` as the song, scheduled at sample positions; the AVPlayer path would need to move into the engine. The click then cannot drift, cannot start late, cannot disagree with the song about speaker delay, and follows speed changes, seeks and loops for free. It is the repo's deferred "Phase F," and it should no longer wait for "the next audio-engine opening." Its urgency was supposed to be decided by the drift log; M2 and M3 now supply that number.

The haptic and visual for playback then hang off the same stream. Each beat's sample position plus the platform's "heard" timestamp gives one time on the device's monotonic clock. Schedule the buzz and the frame against that time. The same idea later extends to recording over a guide: one clock for click, guide, playback and capture.

Bluetooth stays a special case under any design. Keep the tap calibration. Prompt a fresh one per session when the route is Bluetooth, since the delay drifts by tens of ms over 20–30 minutes. Mark grids recorded over Bluetooth as approximate rather than pretending. No other app found does better than that.

### Step 5: the test that proves it, on real phones

Run this on at least one Samsung, one Pixel and one iPhone, before the fix ships and after every native audio build. M3 and M4 are the instruments.

**What remains uncertain.** None of the playback, haptic or visual numbers in this report have been measured on a phone. The padding suspect is plausible and unverified. The 10–60 ms start delay, the speaker-domain gap and the haptic motor delays are estimates from code and platform behaviour. Whether Android's "heard" timestamps include the phone's speaker-protection processing is undocumented. So is the exact delay a scheduled Core Haptics event adds. Step 1 replaces most of these guesses with numbers.

## Conclusion

The useful shift is from "which bug is it?" to "where is the measurement?" SongNook's recording side is already better than most of the field: no other app found re-checks each take against its own click. The trouble lives where nothing is measured, and where two clocks are asked to agree without ever comparing notes. Patching each crossing makes the errors smaller but leaves the sum unknown, and two partial fixes can make the result worse. A click rendered inside the song's stream has no crossing to patch. The device test then becomes a release gate rather than a debugging session.

The order matters as much as the fixes. One afternoon of M1 and M3 decides whether the founder's offset is the file or the player. A small over-the-air update removes the certain faults within days. One native build then fixes the haptic and, if confirmed, the Android padding. The one-clock work should be planned now, not deferred, because it is the only option that reaches the 5 ms target, and the only one that stays right as speed, pitch, loops and layers add more paths.
